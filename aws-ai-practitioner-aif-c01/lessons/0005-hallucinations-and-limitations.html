Hallucinations and the Limitations of Generative AI

In Lesson 4 you learned that a foundation model generates text one token at a time, choosing each token by probability, not by looking up facts. That single mechanism is the root cause of the risk this lesson is about — and of a few others that come along with it.

Hallucinations

A hallucination is when a model generates text that sounds fluent and confident but is factually wrong or entirely made up — a fake citation, a product feature that doesn't exist, a made-up statistic. It happens because the model was never checking facts against the real world; it was only ever predicting "what token is statistically likely to come next," and a wrong-but-plausible-sounding token can be just as likely-looking as a correct one.

Example: a support chatbot confidently tells a customer "this product has a 5-year warranty," when the company has never offered a warranty longer than 2 years. Nothing was hacked or leaked — the model simply generated a plausible-sounding but false sentence.
Hallucinations can't be fully eliminated, but they can be reduced: by grounding the model in real company data instead of relying on what it memorized (a technique called RAG, coming up in Domain 3), and by keeping a human in the loop for anything high-stakes.

Two more limitations worth knowing

Knowledge cutoff
A foundation model only knows what existed in its training data up to a certain date. Ask about something more recent and it either says so, or — worse — hallucinates an answer instead of admitting it doesn't know.
Bias
If the training data itself is skewed (e.g. resumes mostly from one demographic group), the model can learn and repeat that skew — favoring patterns that look like its training data over genuinely better ones.

The jargon, defined

Hallucination
Confident, fluent output that is factually incorrect or fabricated, produced because the model predicts plausible tokens rather than verified facts.
Knowledge cutoff
The date after which a foundation model has no training data, and therefore no built-in knowledge of events.
Bias (in this context)
A skew in a model's outputs that traces back to a skew already present in its training data.
Why this matters for the exam: AIF-C01 loves scenarios where an output is wrong, outdated, or unfairly skewed, and asks you to name which limitation that is — exactly what the quiz below practices.

Practice with exam-style scenarios

1. A customer service chatbot confidently tells a customer that a product has a 5-year warranty, but the company has never offered a warranty longer than 2 years. What is this an example of?
A fluent, confident, but fabricated fact — with no data breach or mislabeled training example involved — is the signature of a hallucination.
2. An employee asks a foundation model about an event that happened last week, and the model says it has no information about anything that recent. What most likely explains this?
The model's knowledge stops at its training data's cutoff date — anything after that, it simply never saw.
3. A hiring-recommendation model, trained mostly on resumes from one demographic group, starts favoring candidates who resemble that group over equally qualified others. What limitation does this illustrate?
The model didn't invent this preference — it learned and repeated a skew that was already present in its training data.
